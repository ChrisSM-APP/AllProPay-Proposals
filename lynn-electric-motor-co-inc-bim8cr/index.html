If You Ever Want to Zero Out the Fee

You are absorbing the card fee today, and this proposal keeps it that way. If down the road you would rather stop paying processing at all, the same account flips to dual pricing with no new application and no new equipment. The posted price covers the card fee, so the card-paying customer covers it and cash or check pays list, and your cost on card sales drops to essentially zero. For a parts counter it is a common setup. No pressure either way, it is just there if you want it.

  • Start where you are. Keep absorbing the fee on interchange-plus, exactly like today, just at a lower cost.
  • Flip whenever you want. Move the card fee to the card-paying customer later with no new paperwork and no new hardware.
  • Cash and check always pay list. Only the customer who chooses to pay by card covers the card fee.
Why This Is the Right Move
  • Every in-person sale today is billed as hand-keyed at roughly 3.5 percent. That is the most expensive way to take a card, on sales that qualify for the cheapest.
  • On interchange plus 0.6 percent and 5 cents, an in-person sale typically costs $1.90 to $2.20 per $100 against about $3.50 today, an estimated $1.30 to $1.60 back on every $100 in card sales.
  • No more hand-keying. Sky taps or dips the card and it posts to QuickBooks, which kills the admin time and the chargeback risk of typing card numbers by hand.
  • The dead terminal gets replaced free. No lease, no rental line, no charge.
  • QuickBooks stays, and there is no contract or cancellation fee. If it does not do what this page says, you walk.

Take a $200 parts sale. Hand-keyed through QuickBooks at about 3.5 percent, it costs about $7.00 to get paid. Tapped on the free terminal at interchange plus 0.6 percent and 5 cents, the same sale typically costs $3.80 to $4.40. That is roughly $2.60 to $3.20 back on every $200 sale, about $1.30 to $1.60 per $100, sale after sale. Your volume varies month to month, so we are not going to invent an annual number for you. Use the estimator above with a typical month, and one recent QuickBooks payout summary turns it into exact dollars.
